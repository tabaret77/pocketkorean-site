Making plans with friends in Korean: from 시간 돼? to 그때 봐

2026-10-22

Phrase from this story: 시간 돼? = Are you free?Search a word

Late autumn is the season for meeting up in Korea: the leaves are turning, the air is crisp and everyone wants one last walk outside before winter. Whether it's a coffee, a hike or dinner, you'll need the same handful of phrases to make a plan. Here they are, in the order a real conversation goes.

Most of these are casual (반말, banmal), the way friends text each other. The polite versions, for classmates or colleagues you're not close to yet, are in the table at the end.

Step 1: Are you free?

  • 이번 주말에 뭐 해? (Ibeon jumare mwo hae?) – What are you doing this weekend?
  • 토요일에 시간 돼? (Toyoire sigan dwae?) – Are you free on Saturday?

시간 (sigan) means time, and 되다 (doeda) here means "to work out" or "to be possible". So 시간 돼? is literally "Does time work?" The polite version is 시간 괜찮으세요? (Sigan gwaenchaneuseyo?) – Is your time okay?

Step 2: Suggest something

Korean has two very handy endings for suggestions:

  • -(으)ㄹ까? – "Shall we…?": 단풍 보러 갈까? (Danpung boreo galkka?) – Shall we go see the autumn leaves?
  • -자 – "Let's…": 맛있는 거 먹으러 가자! (Masinneun geo meogeureo gaja!) – Let's go eat something good!

The -(으)러 가다 in both sentences means "to go (in order) to do something": 보러 가다, to go to see; 먹으러 가다, to go to eat. With it, you can turn almost any activity into a plan: 커피 마시러 갈까? (Keopi masireo galkka?) – Shall we go for a coffee?

Step 3: Fix the time and place

  • 몇 시에 만날까? (Myeot sie mannalkka?) – What time shall we meet?
  • 어디서 볼까? (Eodiseo bolkka?) – Where shall we meet?
  • 강남역 2번 출구에서 보자. (Gangnamyeok ibeon chulgueseo boja.) – Let's meet at Gangnam Station, exit 2.

Notice that Koreans often say 보다 (boda) – to see – for "to meet up", just as English says "see you there". Subway exits, 출구 (chulgu), are the classic meeting point in Seoul, because every exit has a number.

Step 4: Say yes – or no, nicely

Saying yes is easy: 좋아! (Joa!) – Sounds good! Friends also say 콜! (Kol!), from English "call", meaning "Deal!"

Saying no takes a little more care. Koreans rarely give a flat no; they explain and offer another time:

  • 미안, 그날은 안 돼. (Mian, geunareun an dwae.) – Sorry, that day doesn't work.
  • 선약이 있어. (Seonyagi isseo.) – I already have plans.
  • 다음 주는 어때? (Daeum juneun eottae?) – How about next week?

선약 (seonyak) means "an earlier appointment", and it's the polite, no-questions-asked excuse.

Step 5: On the day

  • 그때 봐! (Geuttae bwa!) – See you then!
  • 어디야? (Eodiya?) – Where are you?
  • 조금 늦을 것 같아. 미안! (Jogeum neujeul geot gata. Mian!) – I think I'll be a bit late. Sorry!
  • 거의 다 왔어. (Geoui da wasseo.) – I'm almost there.

다 왔어 literally means "I've come all the way", and it is famously said by people who are still ten minutes away.

The plan that isn't a plan

One phrase needs a warning: 언제 밥 한번 먹자 (Eonje bap hanbeon meokjja [언제 밥 한번 먹짜]) – "Let's grab a meal sometime." It's a friendly goodbye, much like English "We should get coffee sometime", and it often isn't a real invitation. If you want it to happen, follow up with a day: 그럼 다음 주 금요일 어때? (Geureom daeum ju geumyoil eottae?) – Then how about next Friday?
