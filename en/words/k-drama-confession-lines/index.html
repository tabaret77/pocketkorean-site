K-drama confession lines: from 좋아해 to 우리 사귈래?

2026-10-22

Phrase from this story: 우리 사귈래? = Do you want to go out with me?Search a word

Every Korean romance drama builds to the same moment: the 고백 (gobaek), or confession. Someone finally says what they feel, usually under a streetlight, at a bus stop or in the first snow. The lines are short and simple, which makes them perfect for learners. Each one also hides a useful piece of grammar.

These lines are in casual speech (반말, banmal), because by the time characters confess, they're usually close. We add the polite version where it helps.

1. 좋아해 – I like you

좋아해 (joahae) comes from 좋아하다 (joahada) – to like. In Korean dramas, "I like you" is the big confession; 사랑해 (saranghae) – I love you – often comes much later. Politely: 좋아해요 (joahaeyo).

Grammar: the casual present tense is the polite form without 요: 좋아해요 → 좋아해.

2. 나 너 좋아하나 봐 – I think I like you

나 너 좋아하나 봐 (Na neo joahana bwa) is the confession of someone who has only just realised it. -나 보다 means "it seems that", based on evidence: here, the speaker is noticing their own feelings. Compare 비가 오나 봐 (Biga ona bwa) – "Looks like it's raining."

3. 우리 사귈래? – Do you want to go out with me?

우리 사귈래? (Uri sagwillae?) asks someone to be your partner. 사귀다 (sagwida) means to date or to go out with. The ending -(으)ㄹ래? asks what the listener wants to do, like "Do you want to…?" A bolder version uses -자 (let's): 나랑 사귀자 (Narang sagwija) – "Go out with me."

4. 우리 무슨 사이야? – What are we?

우리 무슨 사이야? (Uri museun saiya?) literally asks "What kind of relationship are we?" 사이 (sai) means the space between two things, and also a relationship. This line usually comes after a few dates and no clear answer. A common reply in a happy ending: 이제 사귀는 사이지 (Ije sagwineun saiji) – "We're dating now, of course."

5. 오늘부터 1일 – Day one starts today

오늘부터 1일 (Oneulbuteo iril) means "from today, it's day one". Korean couples count the days of their relationship and celebrate milestones like 100일 (baegil) – the 100th day. Note the pronunciation: 1일 (일일) is said [이릴], iril, because the ㄹ carries over to the next vowel.

6. 데려다줄게 – I'll take you home

데려다줄게 (Deryeodajulge) means "I'll walk you (home)" or "I'll give you a ride". The ending -(으)ㄹ게 is a promise or offer the speaker makes to the listener: 연락할게 (Yeollakalge) – "I'll call you." In dramas, this line is often an excuse to spend five more minutes together.

7. 질투하는 거야? – Are you jealous?

질투하는 거야? (Jiltuhaneun geoya?) is the teasing line when one character gets moody about a rival. 질투하다 (jiltuhada) means to be jealous, and -는 거야? asks "Is it that you're…?", checking the reason behind someone's behaviour. Expect the classic denial: 아니거든! (Anigeodeun!) – "I am not!"

8. 나 기다려 줄 수 있어? – Can you wait for me?

나 기다려 줄 수 있어? (Na gidaryeo jul su isseo?) is said before a long separation: a job abroad, military service, a family crisis. -아/어 주다 means doing something for someone, and -(으)ㄹ 수 있다 means "can". Together: "Can you do me the favour of waiting?" Politely: 기다려 줄 수 있어요? (Gidaryeo jul su isseoyo?)

Real-life note

These lines work outside dramas too, but real confessions are usually less dramatic, and many happen by text. If you hear 우리 사귈래? in real life, a simple 응, 좋아 (Eung, joa) – "Yes, I'd like that" – is all you need.
