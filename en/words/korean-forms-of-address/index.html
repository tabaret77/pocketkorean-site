씨, 님, 선배, 언니: how to address people in Korean

2026-10-23

Phrase from this story: 씨 = Mr/Ms (polite suffix after a name)Search a word

In English, "you" works for everyone from your best friend to the president. Korean doesn't really have that word. 너 (neo) is only for close friends and younger people, and 당신 (dangsin) can sound cold or even confrontational. So Koreans address each other with a name plus a suffix, a title, or a family word, depending on who is older and how close they are. Choosing the right one is half of being polite in Korean.

씨 – for equals

씨 (ssi) goes after a person's name: 민지 씨 (Minji ssi), 김민지 씨 (Gim Minji ssi). It's polite and neutral, roughly "Mr/Ms", and it's what you use with adults of about the same age or rank: classmates in a language school, colleagues at the same level, a new acquaintance.

Two things to watch:

  • Not upwards. As The Korea Herald puts it, adults can use 씨 with each other unless the other person clearly has higher standing. Calling your boss or a much older person 씨 sounds disrespectful.
  • Not with the surname alone. 김 씨 (Gim ssi) can sound cold or condescending, as if you're talking about a worker you don't respect. Use the full name or the given name instead.

님 – the respectful upgrade

님 (nim) is more respectful than 씨. You meet it in three ways:

  • After a title: 선생님 (seonsaengnim, teacher), 사장님 (sajangnim, boss or shop owner), 팀장님 (timjangnim, team leader). At work, Koreans usually call each other by job title plus 님, not by name.
  • For customers: 손님 (sonnim, guest/customer) and 고객님 (gogaengnim, customer). Banks and offices use 님 rather than 씨 so that nobody feels slighted.
  • After a name: 민지 님 (Minji nim). This started with online usernames in the 1990s and is now common in some workplaces and online groups as a polite, neutral option.

When you don't know someone's title, 선생님 is a safe, respectful choice for many adults.

선배 and 후배 – seniors and juniors

선배 (seonbae) is someone who started school, university or a job before you; 후배 (hubae) is someone who started after you. You call a senior 선배 or, more politely, 선배님 (seonbaenim), often with their name: 지수 선배 (Jisu seonbae). Seniors usually call juniors by name. You'll hear these constantly in campus and office dramas.

언니, 오빠, 형, 누나 – family words for friends

Once Koreans are close to someone older, they often switch to "big sister" and "big brother". Which word you use depends on your own gender:

You are… Older woman Older man
female 언니 (eonni) 오빠 (oppa)
male 누나 (nuna) 형 (hyeong)

These are warm and friendly, used for older friends, cousins and even older coworkers you're close to. Don't use them with someone you've just met unless they invite you to. Some women prefer 님 to 오빠 with male schoolmates, the Korea Herald reported, because it feels less loaded.

If you're unsure, ask: 뭐라고 부르면 될까요? (Mworago bureumyeon doelkkayo?) – "What should I call you?" The National Institute of Korean Language's 2020 guide on forms of address, 우리, 뭐라고 부를까요?, makes the same point: terms can change as a relationship changes, and the best one is the one both people are comfortable with.

Getting a stranger's attention

For a waiter or someone on the street, skip names entirely: 저기요 (jeogiyo) – "Excuse me" – or 사장님 for a shop owner.

Useful pattern: 선배님, 질문 하나 해도 돼요? (Seonbaenim, jilmun hana haedo dwaeyo?) – Can I ask you a question? Start with the title instead of "you", and the sentence is polite before you've even asked.
