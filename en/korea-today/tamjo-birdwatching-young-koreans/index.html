Tamjo: why young Koreans are taking up birdwatching

2026-10-24

Phrase from this story: 탐조 = birdwatchingSearch a word

Look up from your phone – and then use it to photograph the bird you just saw. That, in short, is one of Korea's quieter trends this autumn. As reported by the Hankook Ilbo on October 19 and by its sister paper The Korea Times on October 24, 2026, birdwatching is catching on among Korean millennials and Gen Z. In Korean it is called 탐조 (tamjo), and young fans also use the English words 버드 워칭 (beodeu woching) and 버딩 (beoding).

From "uncle hobby" to hip hobby

For a long time, 탐조 had the image of an 아재 취미 (ajae chwimi), a hobby for middle-aged men that needed expensive lenses, heavy field guides and a lot of expert knowledge. That image is fading. The Hankook Ilbo now calls it a 힙한 취미 (hipan chwimi [히판 취미]), a hip hobby.

The numbers on social media show the change. As of mid-October, Instagram had about 144,000 posts tagged #탐조, about 17,000 tagged #탐조기록 (tamjo girok), "birdwatching record," and about 14,000 tagged #탐조생활 (tamjo saenghwal), "birdwatching life." The YouTube channel 새덕후 (KoreanBirder) has 510,000 subscribers. Well-known people have helped too: actress Kim Tae-ri talked about birdwatching as her hobby on Netflix Korea's YouTube channel, and writer Chung Se-rang and model Lee So-ra have also spoken about it.

It is not only a Korean story. The papers point to a survey by the UK's Royal Society for the Protection of Birds, published in May, which found about 750,000 people aged 16 to 29 in Britain watch birds regularly – 1,088% more than in 2018, while the total number of birdwatchers there grew 47% over the same period.

A phone, a park and some patience

Why now? Smartphones have made the hobby cheap to start. A phone camera can take a decent picture, and bird identification apps can suggest a species from its song or the pattern of its feathers. The pandemic was a turning point as well: birdwatching content took off during COVID-19, and the hobby spread quickly among young people around the world.

That is why Korean birdwatching often happens close to home: 도시 탐조 (dosi tamjo), urban birdwatching, and 아파트 탐조 (apateu tamjo), birdwatching in apartment complexes. Park Im-ja, who runs 탐조책방 (tamjo chaekppang [탐조책빵]), described as Korea's first independent bookshop for birdwatching, started watching birds around her own apartment complex during COVID-19 and now leads an apartment birdwatching group. Hyun Ji-yoon, 29, records her outings as comics on the Instagram account 탐조일기 (tamjo ilgi), "birdwatching diary."

Many fans describe it as a 디지털 디톡스 (dijiteol ditokseu), a digital detox. Instead of scrolling or buying things, you stand still, listen and watch – which they say is much less tiring.

Why it matters for learners

탐조 is a Sino-Korean word: 탐 (tam) means to search or explore, as in 탐험 (tamheom), exploration, and 조 (jo) means bird. In everyday speech, though, people simply say 새 (sae), bird, and 새를 보다 (saereul boda), to watch birds. The trend's catchy line – "나랑 새 보러 가지 않을래?" (narang sae boreo gaji aneullae? [아늘래]), "Want to go and see birds with me?" – is a great model for invitations.

A pattern you can use right away: 새 보러 가요 (sae boreo gayo) – "I'm going to see birds." Verb stem + -(으)러 가다 means "to go (somewhere) in order to do something": 밥 먹으러 가요 (bap meogeureo gayo), "I'm going to eat," 꽃 보러 가요 (kkot boreo gayo [꼳 뽀러 가요]), "I'm going to see flowers."
