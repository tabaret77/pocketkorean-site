Every Korean neighbourhood has one: a small, brightly lit restaurant near a school or subway exit, with a long menu on the wall and a big pan of red rice cakes bubbling by the door. This is the **[분식집](/en/dictionary/?q=분식집)** (bunsikjjip [분식찝]), the snack bar. **분식** (bunsik) originally meant food made from flour, but today it covers a whole family of cheap, filling comfort food that students, office workers and late-night drinkers all love. Prices below are as of October 2026.

## The classic menu

**[떡볶이](/en/dictionary/?q=떡볶이)** (tteokbokki [떡뽀끼]) is the heart of it: chewy cylinders of rice cake simmered in a sweet, spicy sauce of red pepper paste, usually with fish cake, spring onion and sometimes a boiled egg. The red version is fairly young. It is said to have been first sold in 1953 in Sindang-dong, Seoul, by a woman named Ma Bok-rim, whose sauce mixed red pepper paste with black bean paste. Long before that, the royal court ate **궁중떡볶이** (gungjung tteokbokki), stir-fried rice cakes with beef and vegetables and no chilli at all. Some shops use rice cakes made of rice, **쌀떡** (ssaltteok), others wheat ones, **밀떡** (miltteok), which spread after the Korean War when flour became widely available. Fans argue about which is better.

**[순대](/en/dictionary/?q=순대)** (sundae) is Korean blood sausage, a pig-intestine casing stuffed mainly with glass noodles and pig's blood. It's mild and soft, and it's sliced and served with salt for dipping. You may be asked whether you want **내장** (naejang), offal such as liver and lung, mixed in.

**[튀김](/en/dictionary/?q=튀김)** (twigim) means fried things: squid, sweet potato, chilli, boiled egg and **김말이** (gimmari), glass noodles rolled in seaweed and deep-fried. The best way to eat them is to dip them in the tteokbokki sauce.

**[김밥](/en/dictionary/?q=김밥)** (gimbap) is rice and fillings rolled in seaweed and sliced. According to the Korea Consumer Agency, one roll cost an average of 3,923 won in Seoul in September 2026, up 8.3% in a year.

You'll also see **어묵** (eomuk), fish cake on skewers in broth, and **라볶이** (rabokki), tteokbokki cooked with instant ramen noodles.

## The perfect combo: 떡튀순

Order tteokbokki, a few fried snacks and some sundae together and you have **떡튀순** (tteoktwisun), short for **떡**볶이, **튀**김 and **순**대. It's the classic set for two or three people sharing, and some shops sell it as one dish.

## How ordering works

- **Pay first, often.** Many snack bars have a touchscreen kiosk or ask you to pay at the counter before the food comes. Look for a menu board with numbers or a screen by the door.
- **Order by portion.** Tteokbokki is sold by the plate, **인분** (inbun), and kimbap by the roll, **줄** (jul): **김밥 한 줄** (gimbap han jul), one roll of kimbap.
- **Choose the heat.** Shops that let you choose usually offer **순한맛** (sunhanmat), mild, **보통맛** (botongmat), medium, and **매운맛** (maeunmat), hot. If the menu doesn't say, ask for it less spicy.
- **Help yourself.** Water, pickled radish and chopsticks are often self-service. Look for a sign saying **셀프** (selpeu).
