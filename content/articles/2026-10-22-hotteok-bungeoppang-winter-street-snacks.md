In Korea, the first really cold evening of the year has a smell: hot oil, brown sugar and toasted red beans. As autumn turns to winter, small stalls appear near subway exits and markets selling **[호떡](/en/dictionary/?q=호떡)** (hotteok), **[붕어빵](/en/dictionary/?q=붕어빵)** (bungeoppang) and other warm snacks. They're cheap, filling and best eaten standing up, with your hands wrapped around a paper cup. Here's what to look for and how to order.

## The big three

**호떡** (hotteok) is a round pancake of soft dough filled with brown sugar, cinnamon and chopped nuts. It is fried on an oily griddle and pressed flat with a metal stamp, so the sugar inside melts into a hot syrup. Take the first bite carefully: the filling can burn your tongue. Busan is known for **씨앗호떡** (ssiat-hotteok), a version split open and stuffed with sunflower and pumpkin seeds.

**붕어빵** (bungeoppang) means "carp bread." It's a crisp, fish-shaped waffle usually filled with sweet red bean paste, **팥** (pat). Custard cream, **슈크림** (syukeurim), is the other classic filling, and Koreans love to argue about which is better and whether to eat the head or the tail first.

**군고구마** (gungoguma) are sweet potatoes roasted in a metal drum or a stone oven until the skin is charred and the inside is soft and sticky. They're sold by weight or by the bag, often next to **군밤** (gunbam), roasted chestnuts.

## A few more to try

- **어묵** (eomuk), fish cake on a skewer, simmered in broth. At many stalls you help yourself, and the broth, **국물** (gungmul), is free to drink from a paper cup. Count your skewers and pay when you finish.
- **계란빵** (gyeranppang), "egg bread": a small sweet loaf baked with a whole egg on top.
- **호두과자** (hodugwaja), walnut-shaped cakes with red bean and walnut inside, sold at highway rest stops and train stations.

## What it costs, and why stalls are harder to find

Prices vary from stall to stall. As of October 2026 there is no official price, but news reports from last winter described bungeoppang typically selling for about 2,000 won for three, with some stalls charging 1,000 won a piece. Older Koreans remember three or even four for 1,000 won.

Stalls have also become harder to find. According to a November 2025 report, the price of red beans rose more than one and a half times in a year, flour and oil stayed expensive, and the number of street vendors in Seoul dropped by more than a fifth, from 6,079 in 2020 to 4,741 in the latest count cited. Many Koreans now use a crowd-sourced map app, **가슴속3천원** (gaseumsok samcheonwon), "3,000 won in my heart," to find the nearest stall. There's even a joke word for living near one: **붕세권** (bungsegwon), a play on **역세권** (yeokssegwon [역쎄권]), "within walking distance of a station."

Bring cash or have a bank-transfer option ready: many small stalls prefer cash, though more now take cards.

## How to order

Point and use a number with **개** (gae), the general counter for things:

- **호떡 두 개 주세요.** (hotteok du gae juseyo.) – Two hotteok, please.
- **붕어빵 천 원어치 주세요.** (bungeoppang cheon woneochi juseyo.) – 1,000 won's worth of bungeoppang, please.
- **팥이에요, 슈크림이에요?** (pachieyo, syukeurimieyo?) – Is it red bean or custard?

**-어치** (-eochi) after an amount of money means "that much worth of," so it's perfect when you don't know how many you'll get.
