Korean series filled three of the top four places on Netflix's weekly Global Top 10 for non-English TV shows. In the chart for October 12–18, 2026, released on Tuesday, October 20, *Doctor X: Mafia in White* stayed at No. 1 with 7.1 million views. Right behind it was the Netflix original **Take Charge of My Heart**, starring Kim Young-kwang and Chae Soo-bin, with 6.4 million views. tvN's *100 Days of Deception* came in at No. 4 with 2.8 million.

## The show watched for the most hours

Netflix ranks shows by views, but its chart also lists the total hours people spent watching – and there the story looks different. *Take Charge of My Heart* was watched for 67.8 million hours in that week, more than twice the 30 million hours of the No. 1 show and the highest figure on the whole non-English list. All 10 episodes, each about an hour long, came out together on October 9, so viewers could watch the whole series at once.

It was the drama's second week in the top 10, and it reached the top 10 in 64 countries, according to Yonhap via Korea JoongAng Daily. *Doctor X* was in the top 10 in 48 countries and No. 1 in seven, including Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam and Singapore.

## A heart that needs a battery

The plot is pure fantasy rom-com. Kim Young-kwang plays Baek Ho-rang, a third-generation [재벌](/en/dictionary/?q=재벌) (jaebeol) heir whose artificial heart is running out of power. Chae Soo-bin plays Bo-bae, a TV writer with a strange problem: she gives an electric shock to anyone she touches. Ho-rang turns out to be immune – and her touch recharges his heart. So the two make a deal, and the business arrangement slowly becomes a romance.

The series is directed by Park Soo-won, known for *Work Later, Drink Now*, and it is based on a popular web novel by Hae Boon. Its English title used to be *Recharge Me*. It streams on Netflix worldwide (as of October 2026).

## Why it matters for learners

The Korean title is **나를 충전해줘** (nareul chungjeonhaejwo), "Recharge me." [충전](/en/dictionary/?q=충전) (chungjeon) is the everyday word for charging something: your phone, your transit card, your battery. Add **하다** and you get the verb **충전하다** (chungjeonhada), to charge. You will hear it all the time in Korea:

- **배터리가 없어요.** (Baeteoriga eopsseoyo. [배터리가 업써요.]) – My battery is dead.
- **휴대폰 충전 좀 해도 돼요?** (Hyudaepon chungjeon jom haedo dwaeyo?) – Can I charge my phone?

Koreans also use 충전 for people. After a holiday or a good meal, someone might say they have "charged" their energy. That is exactly the double meaning in the title: a man whose heart needs a real battery, and a heart that a person fills up.

Another useful word from the plot is [심장](/en/dictionary/?q=심장) (simjang), the heart as a body organ. For the heart as feelings, Korean usually uses **마음** (maeum) instead.

A request pattern straight from the title: **충전해 줘** (chungjeonhae jwo) – "Charge it for me." Verb stem + **-아/어 줘** asks someone to do something for you in casual speech; to a stranger, make it polite with **-아/어 주세요**, as in **충전해 주세요** (chungjeonhae juseyo).
