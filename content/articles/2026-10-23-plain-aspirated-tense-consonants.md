**달** (dal) is the moon, **탈** (tal) is a mask, and **딸** (ttal) is a daughter. To an English ear the first two can sound almost the same, and the third might not sound different at all. But for Korean speakers these are three completely separate words. Korean sorts many of its consonants into sets of three: **plain**, **aspirated** and **tense**. Once you can hear and make the difference, a lot of "why didn't they understand me?" moments disappear.

## The five families

| Plain (예사소리) | Aspirated (거센소리) | Tense (된소리) |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ g/k | ㅋ k | ㄲ kk |
| ㄷ d/t | ㅌ t | ㄸ tt |
| ㅂ b/p | ㅍ p | ㅃ pp |
| ㅈ j | ㅊ ch | ㅉ jj |
| ㅅ s | – | ㅆ ss |

Look at the shapes: hangul shows the family. An extra stroke makes the aspirated letter (ㄱ → ㅋ, ㄷ → ㅌ, ㅈ → ㅊ), and a doubled letter makes the tense one (ㄱ → ㄲ). ㅅ has no aspirated partner, only the tense **ㅆ**.

## How each one is made

**Plain: a light breath.** **[예사소리](/en/dictionary/?q=예사소리)** (yesasori) is relaxed. At the start of a word it comes out with a little air, somewhere between English "g" and "k". Between vowels it becomes softer and voiced: the ㄱ in **아기** (agi) – baby – sounds like a real "g".

**Aspirated: a strong puff.** **거센소리** (geosensori), "rough sound", comes with a big burst of air, stronger than the English "k" in "kite". Try the paper test: hold a tissue in front of your mouth and say **[탈](/en/dictionary/?q=탈)** (tal). The tissue should jump.

**Tense: no air, tight throat.** **[된소리](/en/dictionary/?q=된소리)** (doensori), "hard sound", has almost no puff at all. Tighten your throat as if you were about to lift something heavy, then release the sound sharply. With **딸** (ttal) the tissue should hardly move. Tense consonants also make the following vowel sound a little higher in pitch.

A handy way to practise is to say the three in a row, slowly, with the tissue: **달 – 탈 – 딸**. Little air, lots of air, no air.

## Word sets to practise

| Plain | Aspirated | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| 불 (bul) – fire | 풀 (pul) – grass, glue | 뿔 (ppul) – horn |
| 달 (dal) – moon | 탈 (tal) – mask | 딸 (ttal) – daughter |
| 자다 (jada) – to sleep | 차다 (chada) – to kick; to be cold | 짜다 (jjada) – to be salty |
| 개 (gae) – dog | – | 깨 (kkae) – sesame |
| 기 (gi) – energy | 키 (ki) – height | – |
| 사다 (sada) – to buy | – | 싸다 (ssada) – to be cheap |

The last pair is a classic shopping mix-up: **사요** (sayo) means "I'll buy it", while **싸요** (ssayo) means "it's cheap".

With Halloween around the corner, **탈** is a good word to remember. It's the word for a traditional Korean mask, as in **하회탈** (hahoetal), the name of a famous style of carved wooden mask.

## Hidden tense sounds

Sometimes a plain letter is pronounced tense even though it's written plain. This happens after a final ㄱ, ㄷ or ㅂ sound:

- **학교** (hakgyo) – school → [학꾜]
- **식당** (sikdang) – restaurant → [식땅]
- **입구** (ipgu) – entrance → [입꾸]

Revised Romanization doesn't show this tensing inside a word, so you read "hakgyo" but say something closer to "hak-kyo". The spelling stays plain; only the sound changes.

## Romanization tip

In romanization, the doubled letter is the tense one: **kk, tt, pp, jj, ss**. The single letters **k, t, p, ch** at the start of a syllable are the aspirated ones, and **g, d, b, j** are plain. So **꼬리** (kkori) – tail – has a tense sound, **코** (ko) – nose – an aspirated one, and **고기** (gogi) – meat – a plain one.

| 거센소리 | geosensori | aspirated consonant |
| 된소리 | doensori | tense consonant |

| 다시 말해 주세요 | dasi malhae juseyo | Please say it again |

**이거 ㄲ이에요, ㅋ이에요?** (Igeo ssanggiyeogieyo, kieugieyo?) – Is this ㄲ or ㅋ? Ask a Korean friend this when you're not sure what you heard; letter names like **쌍기역** (ssanggiyeok) for ㄲ and **키읔** (kieuk) for ㅋ are worth learning too.
