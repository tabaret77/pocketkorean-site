One of the first things you learn after moving into a Korean flat is that rubbish is serious business. Everything is sorted, general waste goes into special paid bags, and food scraps have their own system. The word you'll hear most is **분리수거** (bullisugeo), "separate collection." Get it wrong and your bag may simply be left behind, or you could face a fine. Rules are set by each district, so check your own; the basics below are as of October 2026.

## General waste: the paid bags

Ordinary rubbish, **일반 쓰레기** (ilban sseuregi), must go into an official volume-based bag, **[종량제 봉투](/en/dictionary/?q=종량제)** (jongnyangje bongtu). You pay for the bag, so the more you throw away, the more you pay. A few things to know:

- Bags are sold at convenience stores, supermarkets and local marts. Just ask for one at the counter.
- They belong to a specific district: a Gangnam-gu bag can't be used in Jongno-gu. Colours differ from district to district too.
- A 20-litre general bag costs around 480 won, though prices vary by region and size.
- Using a normal plastic bag is a violation. In Seoul's Jongno District, for example, the fine for skipping the official bag or mixing banned items into it can reach 1 million won.

Broken glass, ceramics and other things that can't be burned often go into a separate, sturdier bag. Shoes, umbrellas, rubber and leather go into the normal paid bag.

## Food waste, and what is not food waste

Food scraps, **[음식물 쓰레기](/en/dictionary/?q=음식물)** (eumsingmul sseuregi), are turned into compost and animal feed, which is why they are collected separately. Drain off as much liquid as you can first. Apartment complexes usually have a shared food-waste bin, sometimes an RFID machine that weighs your scraps and charges you by card; villas and smaller buildings often use small food-waste bags sold like the general ones.

The tricky part is what does *not* count as food waste, because animals can't eat it and machines can't process it. Gangnam District's guide puts these in the general bag instead:

- bones from beef, pork and chicken
- shells of clams, oysters, abalone and other shellfish
- hard fruit stones and the shells of walnuts, chestnuts, peanuts and coconuts, plus pineapple skin
- onion, garlic and ginger skins, spring-onion roots and chilli seeds

Eggshells and tea leaves are also commonly excluded. If you're unsure, ask a neighbour or your building manager.

## Recycling

Recycling, **[재활용](/en/dictionary/?q=재활용)** (jaehwaryong), is divided into paper, paper cartons, plastic containers, vinyl and plastic wrap, clear PET bottles, white styrofoam, glass bottles and metal cans. The routine is simple: empty it, rinse it, peel off labels, dry it and flatten it. Anything too dirty to rinse goes into general waste. Batteries go into collection boxes at community centres and subway stations.

Apartments usually collect recycling once a week on a fixed day, so look for a notice near the lifts. Large items such as furniture, **대형폐기물** (daehyeong pyegimul), need to be reported to the district first and given a paid sticker or code.

Good news for newcomers: from 26 October 2026, Jongno District is handing out picture-based sorting guides in Korean, English and Chinese at community centres, and many districts have similar guides online.

**이거 어디에 버려요?** (igeo eodie beoryeoyo?) – Where do I throw this away? Point at the item and ask; at a shop, try **종량제 봉투 20리터 주세요** (jongnyangje bongtu isimniteo juseyo), "A 20-litre rubbish bag, please."
