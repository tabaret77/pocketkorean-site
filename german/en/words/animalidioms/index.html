Today's words · 🐷 Animal idioms

Lucky pigs and crazy birds: 10 German animal idioms

B2C2

In German, a lucky person ‘has pig’ — and someone a bit crazy ‘has a bird’.

You tell a German friend you found fifty euros on the street, and they say: ‘Da hast du aber Schwein gehabt!’ A pig? What pig? Turns out it means ‘Wow, you were lucky!’

Today's ten idioms are ones Germans really use in everyday life. Cats, flies, mosquitoes, hares — picture the animals and the meanings stick. Add these to your German and it instantly sounds more colourful! 🐷

📋 The ten words at a glance

  1. Schwein haben — to be lucky (colloquial)
  2. die Katze aus dem Sack lassen — to let the cat out of the bag
  3. die Katze im Sack kaufen — to buy a pig in a poke
  4. zwei Fliegen mit einer Klappe schlagen — to kill two birds with one stone
  5. aus einer Mücke einen Elefanten machen — to make a mountain out of a molehill
  6. das schwarze Schaf — the black sheep
  7. ein alter Hase sein — to be an old hand
  8. wie Hund und Katze — like cat and dog
  9. einen Vogel haben — (colloquial) to have a screw loose, be nuts
  10. da liegt der Hund begraben — that's the crux of the matter, there's the rub

🧠 Words, examples and memory tricks

01

Schwein haben

[SHVINE HAH-ben]to be lucky (colloquial)

📌 Da hast du aber Schwein gehabt!

→ You were really lucky there!

💡 In Germany pigs mean luck 🐷 One story says the loser of old contests got a pig as a consolation prize.

⚠ No article: Schwein haben, not ein Schwein haben.

02

die Katze aus dem Sack lassen

[dee KAT-suh ows dem ZAK LASS-en]to let the cat out of the bag

📌 Erst kurz vor der Wahl ließ der Kandidat die Katze aus dem Sack.

→ Only shortly before the election did the candidate let the cat out of the bag.

💡 Exactly like English: let the cat out of the bag 🐱

Bonus Similar: ein Geheimnis verraten = to give away a secret

03

die Katze im Sack kaufen

[dee KAT-suh im ZAK KOW-fen]to buy a pig in a poke

📌 Ich kaufe keine Katze im Sack – ich will das Auto erst sehen.

→ I'm not buying a pig in a poke – I want to see the car first.

💡 You thought you bought a hare, but there's a cat in the sack 🐈 English says ‘a pig in a poke’.

⚠ Don't confuse it with aus dem Sack lassen (to reveal a secret)!

04

zwei Fliegen mit einer Klappe schlagen

[tsvy FLEE-gen mit EYE-ner KLAP-uh SHLAH-gen]to kill two birds with one stone

📌 Mit dem Fahrrad zur Arbeit schlage ich zwei Fliegen mit einer Klappe: Sport und Klimaschutz.

→ Cycling to work, I kill two birds with one stone: exercise and climate protection.

💡 Two flies with one swatter 🪰 English kills two birds with one stone.

Bonus A flyswatter is more often called die Fliegenklatsche today.

05

aus einer Mücke einen Elefanten machen

[ows EYE-ner MÜK-uh EYE-nen eh-leh-FAN-ten MAKH-en]to make a mountain out of a molehill

📌 Mach doch aus einer Mücke keinen Elefanten!

→ Don't make a mountain out of a molehill!

💡 Turning a mosquito into an elephant 🐘 English makes a mountain out of a molehill.

Bonus Often used as a command: Mach aus einer Mücke keinen Elefanten!

06

das schwarze Schaf

[dass SHVART-suh SHAHF]the black sheep

📌 Er galt lange als das schwarze Schaf der Familie.

→ For a long time he was regarded as the black sheep of the family.

💡 Same as English: the black sheep 🐑

Bonus Usually ‘of the family’: das schwarze Schaf der Familie

07

ein alter Hase sein

[ine AL-ter HAH-zuh zine]to be an old hand

📌 In der Kommunalpolitik ist er ein alter Hase.

→ He's an old hand at local politics.

💡 An old hare that's escaped every hunter 🐇 = an old hand.

Bonus The opposite: der Neuling = a newcomer

08

wie Hund und Katze

[vee HOONT oont KAT-suh]like cat and dog

📌 Die beiden Nachbarn leben seit Jahren wie Hund und Katze.

→ The two neighbors have been fighting like cat and dog for years.

💡 English says cat and dog — German puts the dog first 🐶

⚠ Keep the German order: wie Hund und Katze, not wie Katze und Hund.

09

einen Vogel haben

[EYE-nen FOH-gel HAH-ben](colloquial) to have a screw loose, be nuts

(hat einen Vogel – hatte einen Vogel – hat einen Vogel gehabt)

📌 Der hat doch einen Vogel, für ein Auto so viel Geld auszugeben!

→ He must have a screw loose, spending so much money on a car!

💡 There's a bird living in your head = you're nuts 🐦 Du hast doch einen Vogel!

⚠ It's rude. The matching gesture — tapping your forehead at another driver — can even get you fined as an insult.

10

da liegt der Hund begraben

[dah leekt dair HOONT beh-GRAH-ben]that's the crux of the matter, there's the rub

📌 Pflegekräfte werden schlecht bezahlt – da liegt der Hund begraben.

→ Care workers are badly paid – that's the crux of the matter.

💡 ‘That's where the dog is buried’ = that's the real problem 🐕

Bonus Similar: der Knackpunkt = the crucial point

🇩🇪 A spoonful of German culture

In Germany the pig is a symbol of good luck. At New Year, people often give little marzipan pigs (Glücksschwein), along with other lucky charms (Glücksbringer) like chimney sweeps, four-leaf clovers and red-and-white toadstools.

The same idea often uses a different animal in each language. English fights like cats and dogs; German lives wie Hund und Katze. English kills birds with a stone; German swats flies. Comparing them makes them much easier to remember.

And einen Vogel haben has its own gesture: tapping your forehead with your finger. It's genuinely insulting, and doing it at another driver can land you in trouble. Know it — but don't use it! 🐦

✅ Quick quiz

Q. Your friend just found fifty euros on the street. How do you react?

  1. ① Du hast einen Vogel!
  2. ② Da hast du aber Schwein gehabt!
  3. ③ Da liegt der Hund begraben.
Show the answer

② Da hast du aber Schwein gehabt!
Schwein haben = to be lucky, so this means ‘You were really lucky!’

If anything lucky happens to you today, say ‘Ich habe Schwein gehabt!’ out loud. One animal idiom and your German comes alive! 🍀
